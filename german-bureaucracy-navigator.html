The challenge

The information exists. The path through it doesn't.

Moving to Germany comes with a set of administrative tasks: registering an address, arranging health insurance, enrolling at a university, applying for a residence permit, dealing with letters about tax IDs and the broadcasting fee.

Most of these are documented somewhere, often well. But each one is documented by the institution that owns it — a city office, an immigration authority, an insurer, a university — and each explains its own step. Nobody is responsible for explaining how the steps fit together for one particular person.

So the problem I want to look at isn't missing information. It's that a newcomer has to assemble their own process out of separate sources, usually in a second or third language, while unsure which parts apply to them.

The questions a single web page rarely answers

  • What applies to my situation?Requirements differ by citizenship, purpose of stay, city, and timing.
  • What should I do first?Some steps need a document that only another step produces.
  • What do I need to bring, and where?Document lists and responsible offices are described separately, in official terms.
  • What have I done, and what's next?There is no shared record of progress across institutions.

Fragmented sources

Each authority covers its own step. The connections between steps are left to the reader.

Unfamiliar terminology

Terms like Meldebescheinigung or Wohnungsgeberbestätigung don't translate cleanly, and translations vary between sites.

Hidden dependencies

One step may require the output of another. This often only becomes visible at the appointment.

Unclear ownership

Finding which office is responsible, in which city, is a task in itself.

Deadlines

Some steps are time-bound, which raises the cost of working out the order by trial and error.

No two cases alike

A non-EU student and an EU employee face different sets of tasks. Generic checklists fit neither exactly.

This is my reading of the problem space from the outside. I'm an international student in Germany myself, which is a reason to treat my own experience as a source of bias to check, not as evidence.

Problem framing

Starting question

Initial framing — expected to change after research

How might we help newcomers understand which administrative tasks apply to them, what order to complete them in, and what they need for each step?

In scope

  • Helping someone see their own set of tasks and the order between them
  • Explaining each task in plain language, with the official term kept visible
  • Pointing to the authoritative source for every statement
  • Keeping track of what's done, in progress, and blocked

Deliberately out of scope

  • Legal or immigration advice, or decisions about individual cases
  • Submitting applications or booking appointments on someone's behalf
  • Replacing official portals — the concept sits on top of them and links back
  • Collecting documents or identity data
Target users

Who this could be for

These are the groups I intend to recruit from and design for. They are not personas and not research participants — I haven't spoken to anyone yet. The point of listing them is that their administrative journeys likely differ, and the product has to handle that difference without asking for more than it needs.

International students

Journey likely shaped by enrolment deadlines and the link between health insurance and enrolment.

Newly arrived employees

Journey likely shaped by a start date, payroll needs such as a tax ID, and sometimes employer support.

Researchers

Often hosted by an institution with its own welcome service; may sit between the student and employee paths.

People immigrating long-term

Broader set of tasks, often without an institution acting as a guide.

Accompanying partners and family

Their steps may depend on someone else's status and paperwork, which adds another layer of dependency.

Open question

Which of these groups has the least existing support? That would decide who to design for first. To be validated

Initial hypotheses

What I currently believe, and how I'd find out

Hypotheses — none of these have been tested

  1. Users may struggle more with the sequence of tasks than with finding individual pieces of information.

    Would support it
    People describe knowing what a step was but doing things in an order that cost them time.
    Would weaken it
    People mostly describe not being able to find or understand a single step.

  2. Users may be uncertain whether information applies to their particular situation.

    Would support it
    People cross-check the same question across several sources or ask others "is this the same for me?"
    Would weaken it
    People trust the first official page they find and act on it.

  3. Users may need stronger visibility of prerequisites and dependencies.

    Would support it
    Accounts of appointments that failed, or had to be repeated, because something was missing.
    Would weaken it
    Prerequisites were clear in advance; the difficulty was elsewhere (e.g. getting an appointment).

  4. Users may value seeing progress through the administrative journey.

    Would support it
    People already keep their own lists, notes, or folders to track status.
    Would weaken it
    People treat each task as a one-off and feel no need for an overview.

  5. Users may need both simplified explanations and links back to authoritative sources.

    Would support it
    People use informal sources to understand, then go to official ones to confirm.
    Would weaken it
    People rely on only one kind of source and don't miss the other.
Research questions

What the research needs to answer

  1. How do newcomers currently organise their administrative tasks? relates to H4
  2. Which steps create the greatest uncertainty, and why those? H1, H3
  3. How do people decide whether a piece of information applies to them? H2
  4. What causes people to lose confidence during a process? H2, H3
  5. How do language and official terminology affect comprehension? H5
  6. Which tools, people, or workarounds do they rely on today? H4, H5
  7. What do people need to know before they feel ready to begin a task? H3
Research plan

Five studies, in the order they'd inform each other

The plan moves from understanding to evaluating. The first two studies are about what people actually did; nothing gets designed in detail until those are synthesised. All of it is still ahead.

  1. 1

    Semi-structured interviews

    Planned
    Purpose
    Understand how people approached their first months of paperwork, in their own words. Answers RQ1–RQ6.
    Proposed setup
    5–8 people who arrived in roughly the last two years, mixed across student, employee, and researcher. About 45 minutes each.
    Output
    Transcripts, coded and grouped through affinity mapping into themes.
  2. 2

    Walkthrough of a recent process

    Planned
    Purpose
    Recall is unreliable for sequences. Ask participants to retrace one real process using their own emails, notes, and bookmarks.
    Proposed setup
    Part of the interview session, or a follow-up with a subset of participants. Participants choose what to show.
    Output
    Actual task sequences, including detours and repeats, to compare against my assumed journey.
  3. 3

    Comparative analysis

    Planned
    Purpose
    See how existing resources handle personalisation, sequence, and sourcing — and where they stop.
    Proposed setup
    Attempt the same three tasks on each resource and record what happens, using the framework further down.
    Output
    A filled-in comparison matrix with evidence per cell.
  4. 4

    Information architecture exercises

    Planned
    Purpose
    Check whether people group and order tasks the way the concept does, and which labels they understand.
    Proposed setup
    Card sorting on task names (German term vs. plain description), followed by a tree test on the resulting structure.
    Output
    A revised task structure and naming scheme.
  5. 5

    Prototype usability testing

    Planned
    Purpose
    Find out whether people can identify their next step, and explain why a step is blocked, using the prototype.
    Proposed setup
    Moderated, task-based sessions with think-aloud. Scenario profiles are assigned so no one has to share their own status.
    Output
    Observed problems ranked by severity, plus the measures listed under proposed metrics.

Ethics note for the plan: participation would be voluntary and anonymised, and people would never be asked to show documents or disclose their residence status beyond what they choose to mention.

Ecosystem

Not one form. Several institutions, loosely connected.

Simplified illustration — not official guidance

The version people are usually told

  1. Arrivalin Germany
  2. Addresshousing secured
  3. Anmeldungaddress registration
  4. Residencepermit-related steps
  5. Setupbank, insurance, university, employment

A line like this is a useful first orientation, and it is also misleading. Steps branch depending on who you are, some run in parallel, and each belongs to a different organisation with its own website, vocabulary, and appointment system. Actual requirements vary by person and by place.

Enrolment, and often an international office that offers guidance.

Tax identification number, which employers need for payroll.

Broadcasting fee serviceBeitragsservice

The household broadcasting contribution (Rundfunkbeitrag).

Employer or host institute

Contract, and sometimes onboarding or relocation support.

Landlord and bank

Private actors whose documents the public processes depend on.

Office names and responsibilities differ between cities and federal states. Mapping this properly for one pilot city is part of the comparative analysis.

Current journey

  4. Gather documents

    Trying to
    Collect everything needed.
    Assumed friction

    To ask in research

    Trying to

    Assumed friction

    To ask in research

  6. Complete the process

    Trying to
    Get through the appointment or form.
    Assumed friction
    Language at the counter; unfamiliar forms.
    To ask in research
    What happened that you didn't expect?

  7. Determine the next task

    Trying to
    Understand what this step unlocked.
    Assumed friction
    No one says what comes next.
    To ask in research

Design principles

Eight working principles

Derived from the framing, not from findings — to be validated
01

Show the next action, not everything at once.

A full list of obligations is accurate and overwhelming. Lead with one step; keep the rest one tap away.

From H1
02

Explain why a task matters.

People are more likely to act on a step when they know what it unlocks or what happens without it.

From H3
03

Make dependencies visible.

If something can't be done yet, say what it's waiting on, in a sentence.

From H3
04

Keep authoritative sources accessible.

A simplified explanation is a way in, never a replacement. The official page is always linked.

From H5
05

Separate required from recommended.

Mixing obligations with tips makes both harder to trust.

From H2
06

Never hide uncertainty.

Where a rule varies or the content may be out of date, say so plainly instead of sounding confident.

From H2
07

Design for multilingual comprehension.

Keep the German term next to its explanation; that's the word on the form and at the counter.

From H5
08

Minimise personal data.

Ask only what changes the journey, explain why it's asked, and don't keep it longer than needed.

Constraint
Product concept

A structured journey, not a chatbot

The concept has four parts: a short onboarding, a personal dashboard, a detail page per task, and a view of how tasks depend on each other.

I chose structure over conversation on purpose. The core need, as framed, is orientation: seeing the whole, knowing where you are in it. A list with states can be scanned, returned to, and checked against a source. A chat answer can't, and it's harder to tell when it's wrong.

The demo below is a working sketch of that idea. Change the answers on the left and the journey on the right recalculates.

Interactive concept sketch Demo data — nothing you select is stored or sent

1 · Onboarding

What brings you to Germany?

Why we ask: study, work, and family routes involve different steps.

Have you completed enrolment at your university?

Only asked because you chose "Study".

Are you an EU/EEA or Swiss citizen?

Why we ask: it decides whether residence-permit steps appear. We don't ask for your nationality.

Do you already have a place to live?

Why we ask: several steps can't start without an address.

Not asked: name, exact nationality, address, passport or visa details. In the full concept a city would be chosen too, since offices and sources are local.

3 · Dependency view

Select a step to see what it's waiting on and what it unlocks. The relationship is always stated in words as well, so it doesn't depend on reading the diagram.

  • needed first
  • selected
  • unlocked by it

The steps and dependencies in this demo are simplified examples chosen to show the interaction. They are not a verified description of German administrative requirements.

Progress states

Four states a person sets themselves, and one the system derives.

  • Not started
  • Preparing
  • Appointment booked
  • Completed
  • Blocked

"Blocked" isn't selectable. It appears when a prerequisite is unfinished and always names it, so the state explains itself. Whether "Preparing" and "Appointment booked" are the right intermediate states is a question for the walkthroughs. To be validated

Decisions I can defend, and their cost

  • One next step on top. Cost: people who want to plan ahead need the full list, so it stays visible below.
  • Required and recommended are separate lists. Cost: the boundary is sometimes fuzzy and has to be decided per task.
  • Reasons shown beside each question. Cost: longer onboarding screens, in exchange for trust.
  • Coarse profile only. Cost: less precise journeys; some cases will need "check with the office" instead of an answer.

4 · Task detail

Every task follows the same template, so people learn where to look. Sources sit beside the content instead of at the bottom of the page.

Underneath: tasks as structured data

The demo runs on a small rule model. Each task records when it applies, what it requires, who owns it, and where its content comes from. The dashboard, the blocked states, and the dependency view are all computed from that one structure.

This matters for feasibility more than for the interface. Content is the expensive part of this product: someone has to maintain each task per city and re-check its sources. Making sources and lastChecked required fields means a task without a verified source can't be published.

// one task, as the demo models it
{
  id: "residence-permit",
  title: "Apply for a residence permit",
  kind: "required",
  appliesWhen: { euCitizen: false },
  requires: ["anmeldung", "health-insurance"],
  authority: "Immigration office",
  sources: [ /* official URLs */ ],
  lastChecked: null  // placeholder
}

Constraints and edge cases I'm aware of

Content upkeep

Rules change and differ locally. The realistic first version covers one city and a handful of tasks, done properly.

Situations that change

A student becomes an employee; a partner arrives later. The journey has to be editable without starting over.

Cases the model can't express

Some situations won't fit the questions. The product should say "we can't tell — ask this office" instead of guessing.

Future opportunity

Where AI could help, and where it shouldn't decide

Exploration — not part of the core concept

My background is in computer science and AI, so it would be easy to make this an AI product. I don't think it should be one. The journey itself needs to be deterministic and checkable. Language models are a possible layer on top for one thing they're good at: making dense official text easier to read.

Where it could help

  • Rephrasing an official paragraph in simpler language, next to the original
  • Explaining a German administrative term in the user's language
  • Answering a question using passages retrieved from official sources
  • Summarising the documents a source page lists
  • Explaining why a step appears in someone's journey, based on the rule that triggered it

What could go wrong

  • Hallucination. A fluent, wrong answer about a deadline or document.
  • Outdated information. A model's own knowledge has no date on it.
  • Real consequences. A missed deadline or failed appointment costs the user, not the product.
  • Privacy. People may type sensitive details about their status into a free-text box.
  • Source reliability. Retrieval is only as good as the set of sources it searches.

The approach I'd propose: grounded in sources, or silent

  1. 1 · QuestionAsked in the context of a specific task, not an open chat.
  2. 2 · RetrieveSearch only a curated, dated set of official sources.
  3. 3 · Answer with evidenceShow the quoted passage, its link, and when it was last checked.
  4. 4 · Or declineIf nothing relevant is found, say so and point to the responsible office.

The model explains and translates; it never adds or removes steps from a journey. That stays with the rule model, where a person can review it. Whether simplified text actually improves comprehension, or just confidence, would need its own study.

Privacy & trust

Sensitive situations need a cautious product

Residence status, family situation, and employment are things people have good reason to be careful with. A tool in this space earns trust by asking for little and being open about what it knows and doesn't.

Data minimisation

A handful of coarse answers is enough to shape a journey. No names, document numbers, or uploads.

No unnecessary storage

Proposed default: the profile and progress stay on the person's device, with no account required.

Transparency

Every question states why it's asked. Every step can show which answer caused it to appear.

Authoritative sourcing

Each statement links to the official source and shows when it was last checked.

Communicating uncertainty

"Typically", "varies by city", and "we can't tell" are valid outputs. Stale content is flagged, not hidden.

Accessibility and language

Plain language, keyboard and screen-reader support, and translated explanations with the German term kept alongside.

These are design intentions. A real service would also need a proper data-protection review, which is outside what a concept study can claim.
